Dealers love to talk about the monthly payment. This auto loan calculator shows you the whole picture — the amount financed, sales tax, total interest and what the car really costs — before you sign.

Getting the real cost of a car loan

Dealers often lead with the monthly payment, because a low number is easy to say yes to. The better way to shop is to settle the out-the-door price first — vehicle price plus sales tax, title, registration and dealer fees, minus your trade-in and down payment — and then decide how to finance that amount. This calculator follows that order so you can see each piece separately.

How to use the auto loan calculator

  1. Enter the car price, your down payment and any trade-in value.
  2. Enter your state sales tax rate.
  3. Enter the APR and choose a loan term.
  4. Press Calculate.

The formula

Amount financed = price + sales tax − down payment − trade-in

Worked example

A $35,000 car, 7% sales tax, an $8,000 trade-in and $3,000 down at 7.5% APR:

  • In states that tax the price after the trade-in, tax is charged on $27,000, and you finance about $25,890. Over 60 months that is about $519 a month.
  • In states that tax the full price, tax is charged on $35,000 and you finance about $26,450 — about $530 a month.

Most states give the trade-in tax credit, but some do not, so check your state's rule. It can make a difference of several hundred dollars.

Case study: 0% APR versus a $2,500 rebate

You are financing $30,000 over 60 months. The manufacturer offers either 0% APR or a $2,500 cash rebate, not both.

  • 0% APR: $30,000 ÷ 60 = $500 a month, total paid $30,000.
  • Rebate plus a 6.5% credit union loan: finance $27,500 at 6.5% for 60 months = $538 a month, total paid $32,284. The 0% deal wins by $2,284.
  • Rebate plus a 4.5% loan: $513 a month, total $30,761. The 0% deal still wins, by $761.

The rebate only comes out ahead when the interest on the reduced balance is less than the rebate. For these numbers that happens below roughly 3.5% APR over 60 months. Run your own quote in the calculator with the price lowered by the rebate, and remember that 0% offers usually require top-tier credit and may be limited to shorter terms.

Being "upside down" (owing more than the car is worth) matters if the car is totaled or you want to sell early. A larger down payment, a shorter term or GAP insurance reduces that risk.

Ways to lower the cost

  • Get pre-approved by a bank or credit union before visiting the dealer, so you have a rate to compare.
  • Negotiate price, trade-in and financing separately. Combining them makes it easy to give back savings in another place.
  • Watch add-ons (extended warranties, protection packages). They are often rolled into the loan and charged interest.
  • Check the APR, not only the rate. Special 0% offers may require giving up a cash rebate — run both options.

Car buyers' questions about loan math

How much car can I afford?
A common rule of thumb is to keep total car costs (payment, insurance, fuel) under about 15–20% of take-home pay, and the loan term at 60 months or less.

Are dealer fees included?
Add documentation, title and registration fees to the price field. They vary by state and dealer.

Is a 0% offer always best?
Not if it replaces a large rebate. Compare: 0% on the full price versus a rebate plus your own bank's rate.

Is a 72- or 84-month loan a good idea?
It lowers the payment, but you pay more interest and may be “upside down” for years. 60 months or less is usually safer.

Does the trade-in reduce sales tax?
In most US states, yes — tax is charged on the price minus the trade-in. A few states do not allow this credit.
