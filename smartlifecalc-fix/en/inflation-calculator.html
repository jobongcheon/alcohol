Inflation slowly raises prices and shrinks what your money can buy. Use this calculator to see what things will cost in the future, or how much your savings will really be worth.

What inflation does to your money over time

Inflation is the general rise in prices. When it runs at 3% a year, something that costs $100 today costs about $103 next year. Over long periods the effect compounds just like interest, which is why it matters so much for retirement planning, salaries and long-term savings.

How to use the inflation calculator

  1. Choose future cost or future value of money.
  2. Enter the amount, an average inflation rate and the number of years.
  3. Press Calculate.

The formula

Future cost = today’s price × (1 + inflation)years  |  future value = amount ÷ (1 + inflation)years

Worked example

At 3% a year for 20 years, prices rise by a factor of about 1.81. Something that costs $100 today would cost roughly $181. Seen the other way, $100,000 saved in cash today would buy only what about $55,400 buys now. By the Rule of 72, prices double in about 72 ÷ 3 = 24 years.

Which price index adjusts your benefits, taxes and bonds

"Inflation" in the news usually means the CPI-U, but several official indexes exist and each one drives a different part of your finances.

IndexPublished byWhat it adjusts
CPI-U (all urban consumers)Bureau of Labor StatisticsHeadline inflation; Series I savings bond rates; many rent and contract escalators
CPI-W (wage earners)Bureau of Labor StatisticsSocial Security cost-of-living adjustment, from the third-quarter average
Chained CPI (C-CPI-U)Bureau of Labor StatisticsFederal income tax brackets and the standard deduction
Core CPIBureau of Labor StatisticsCPI without food and energy; used to read the trend, not to index payments
PCE price indexBureau of Economic AnalysisThe Federal Reserve's 2% target

The gaps between them look small, often a few tenths of a point a year, but they compound: an index that runs 0.3 points lower for 30 years ends about 8.5% lower, one reason the chained CPI was chosen for tax brackets. When you set a rate here, match it to the money you are planning: CPI-W for Social Security, CPI-U for almost everything else.

Real vs nominal returns

If your savings earn 4% but inflation is 3%, your purchasing power grows by only about 1%. This "real" return is what matters for long-term goals. Money in a checking account earning nothing loses value every year.

Where inflation shows up in planning

  • Salary: a 3% raise in a year of 3% inflation keeps you even, not ahead.
  • Retirement: estimate future expenses in today's dollars, then grow them with inflation.
  • Big goals: college costs and healthcare have often risen faster than overall inflation.

Inflation questions from savers and retirees

What rate should I use?
The U.S. Federal Reserve targets 2% over the long run; many planners use 2.5–3% to be cautious.

Where do official figures come from?
The Consumer Price Index (CPI) published by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (bls.gov).

What has US inflation averaged?
Over the past century US consumer prices have risen about 3% a year on average, with big swings in some decades.

Where can I find official inflation data?
The US Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the Consumer Price Index (CPI) every month at bls.gov.
