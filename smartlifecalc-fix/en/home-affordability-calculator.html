Before you fall in love with a house, find out what price fits your budget. This calculator uses the same debt-to-income rules many lenders use to estimate how much house you can afford.

How much house is comfortable, not just possible

Lenders mostly look at two ratios. The front-end ratio is your total housing payment (principal, interest, taxes, insurance, PMI, HOA) divided by gross monthly income; many lenders like it at or below about 28%. The back-end ratio adds all other monthly debt payments — car, student loans, minimum card payments — and is often capped around 36% to 43%. This calculator uses both and reports the lower result, so you see a price that passes both tests.

How to use the home affordability calculator

Worked example: $90,000 income

Gross monthly income is $7,500. At 28%, the housing budget is about $2,100. If taxes and insurance run about $500 a month, $1,600 is left for principal and interest. At 6.5% for 30 years, that supports a loan of roughly $253,000. With 10% down, that points to a home price around $280,000. If you also have a $450 car payment, the back-end limit may lower that number further.

Property tax and HOA dues quietly shrink the price you can afford

Two $350,000 houses can carry very different monthly costs, and the calculator counts every dollar of tax and dues against the same 28% ceiling.

  • At a 0.6% property tax rate, tax is $350,000 × 0.006 ÷ 12 = $175 a month.
  • At 2.0%, it is $583 a month, a difference of $408.
  • At 6.5% over 30 years, each $1,000 borrowed costs about $6.32 a month, so $408 a month supports about $64,500 of loan.

In other words, the buyer in the high-tax county qualifies for roughly $64,000 less house on the same income. A $300 monthly HOA fee works the same way and trims about $47,000 from the loan. Before you compare listings, pull the actual tax bill from the county assessor's site and the HOA budget from the seller's disclosures, then enter those real figures rather than a national average.

What changes the result the most

  • Interest rate – each 1 percentage point higher reduces the affordable loan by roughly 10%.
  • Other debts – paying off a car loan before buying can raise your budget more than a raise would.
  • Property tax – two homes at the same price can have very different monthly costs in different counties.
  • Down payment – 20% removes PMI on most conventional loans.

Leave room for real life

The ratios don't know about childcare, retirement saving or the roof that will need replacing. A common budgeting rule is to set aside 1–2% of the home's value each year for maintenance, and to keep an emergency fund after closing. Many buyers choose a price below their approval to keep that cushion.

Six checks before you set a price ceiling

  1. Run both ratios and keep the lower result. A $450 car payment can cap you under the 36% back-end test even when the 28% housing test passes.
  2. Count closing costs and reserves against your savings. With $60,000 saved, 3% closing costs on a $350,000 home ($10,500) and $9,000 of reserves leave $40,500 for the down payment, about 11.6%, so PMI applies.
  3. Test the payment against take-home pay. A $2,100 housing payment is 28% of $7,500 gross but about 37% of a $5,700 paycheck.
  4. Stress-test the rate. Rerun the numbers 1 percentage point higher; if the result no longer fits, you are relying on a rate lock holding.
  5. Budget maintenance. At 1–2% of value a year, a $350,000 home needs $290–$580 a month set aside.
  6. Check the loan program's own limits. FHA and other programs use their own ratio caps and insurance costs, so a lender's quote can differ from the 28/36 estimate here.

Affordability questions buyers raise

Gross or take-home pay?
Lenders use gross (before tax). For your own budget, check the payment against take-home pay too.

Do closing costs matter?
Yes — they are often 2–5% of the price and are paid in cash on top of the down payment.

Does my credit score change affordability?
Indirectly: a better score gets a lower rate, which raises the loan you can afford at the same payment.

What is the 28/36 rule?
Housing costs should be no more than 28% of gross income, and all debt payments no more than 36%.

*How was the table estimated?
It assumes no other debt, 20% down, a 6.5% 30-year loan and 1.5% yearly tax and insurance.
