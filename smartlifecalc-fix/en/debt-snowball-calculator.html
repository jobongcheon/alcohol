Snowball pays the smallest balance first; avalanche pays the highest APR first. Freed-up payments roll into the next debt.

If you have several debts — credit cards, a car loan, student loans — the order you pay them off matters. This calculator compares the two most popular strategies, the debt snowball and the debt avalanche, using your real numbers.

Snowball or avalanche: which one should you use?

Both methods have the same basic rule: pay the minimum on every debt, then put every extra dollar toward one target debt. When that debt is gone, roll its payment into the next one. The only difference is the order. The snowball targets the smallest balance first; the avalanche targets the highest interest rate first.

What the minimum payment warning box is telling you

Since the Credit CARD Act of 2009, every monthly credit card statement must show two things: how long it would take to clear the balance paying only the minimum, and the monthly amount that would clear it in 36 months. That box is a one-debt version of this tool, so it is worth reading.

Take the $6,000 card at 24% from the example above. Many issuers set the minimum at 1% of the balance plus that month's interest, with a floor of around $25. Paying only that:

  • The first payment is about $180 ($60 toward the balance, $120 of interest).
  • Each payment shrinks as the balance falls, so the balance barely moves in later years.
  • The card takes roughly 21 years to clear and costs about $10,900 in interest.

The 36-month figure on the same statement would be about $235 a month, for roughly $2,470 in interest. Enter $235 as that card's payment here to see how much the whole stack speeds up.

How to choose

  • Choose avalanche if your high-rate debt is large and you are motivated by numbers. It always costs the least.
  • Choose snowball if you have several small balances or have tried and stopped before. Quick wins keep many people going.
  • A hybrid works too: clear one or two tiny balances first, then switch to the highest rate.

Tips that make either method work

  • Keep the total monthly amount the same even as debts disappear — that is the "snowball".
  • Put windfalls (tax refunds, bonuses) toward the target debt.
  • Don't take on new debt while paying off old debt; consider freezing cards you are paying down.

Four checks before moving a balance to a 0% card

  1. Price the transfer fee against the interest. A 3% fee on $6,000 is $180; at 24% APR that balance costs about $120 in interest in the first month alone, so the fee is repaid in under two months.
  2. Divide the new balance by the promo months. $6,180 over an 18-month 0% period needs $343 a month. If you cannot commit to that, whatever is left rolls onto the regular rate.
  3. Confirm it is a true 0% APR, not deferred interest. Store financing that says "no interest if paid in full" charges all the back interest if even $1 remains when the promotion ends. Credit card balance-transfer offers normally do not.
  4. Keep new purchases off the transfer card. New charges may lose their grace period while a transferred balance is open. In the tool, enter the transferred debt at 0% with the $343 payment and let the avalanche target your remaining high-rate cards.

What readers ask about paying off debt

Should mortgages be included?
Most people leave the mortgage out and focus on consumer debt first, because mortgage rates are usually lower and the term is long.

What if two debts have the same rate?
Pay the smaller one first; you free up its minimum sooner.

Is debt consolidation better?
It can be, if the consolidation loan has a lower APR and you don't run up the cards again. Run the loan calculator to compare.

Which is better, snowball or avalanche?
Avalanche always costs the same or less interest. Snowball can feel more motivating because small debts disappear quickly.

Should I include my mortgage?
Usually no. Most people focus on consumer debt first and keep the mortgage on its normal schedule.
