Compound interest means you earn interest on your interest. Given enough time, it turns small regular savings into a large balance. See how your money can grow with this calculator.

How compound interest really works

With simple interest you earn only on what you deposited. With compound interest, the interest you earned is added to the balance and starts earning interest too. The effect is small in the first years and very large later — which is why time matters more than almost anything else in saving.

Worked example: $10,000 plus $300 a month at 7%

After 20 years, monthly compounding grows the account to about $196,700. You put in $82,000 yourself ($10,000 plus $300 × 240 months); the other $114,700 is growth. In the last few years, the account earns more each year than you contribute.

Case study: $300 a month from age 25 versus age 35

Two savers each put $300 a month into an account earning 7% compounded monthly and stop at 65. The only difference is when they start.

  • Starts at 35 (30 years, $108,000 deposited): about $366,000 at 65.
  • Starts at 25 (40 years, $144,000 deposited): about $787,000 at 65.

The early saver deposits $36,000 more but ends with roughly $421,000 more. For the late starter to catch up by 65, the monthly deposit would have to be about $645, more than double. The reason is that the earliest deposits spend 30 to 40 years compounding: at 7% with monthly compounding, a dollar left alone for 40 years grows to about $16, while a dollar given 30 years grows to about $8. Try the same comparison at 5%; the gap narrows, but the early start still wins by a wide margin.

Three levers you control

  • Start earlier – ten extra years can matter more than a higher contribution.
  • Contribute regularly – automatic monthly deposits remove the need to decide each month.
  • Keep costs low – a 1% yearly fee can take a large share of long-term growth.

Be realistic about the rate

Savings accounts and CDs pay a known rate. Stock and bond returns are not guaranteed and vary year to year; long-run averages are only a guide. Try a lower rate too, and remember inflation: a 7% return with 3% inflation is about 4% in real purchasing power.

Two things to double-check

  • Fees and taxes reduce real returns; low-cost index funds keep more working for you.

Five things to check before chasing a higher rate

  1. Is the account insured? FDIC coverage at banks and NCUA coverage at credit unions each protect up to $250,000 per depositor, per institution, per ownership category. Money market mutual funds, stocks and bonds are not insured.
  2. Is the number an APY? Compare APY with APY. A 5% rate compounded daily is 5.13% APY; compounded monthly it is 5.12%.
  3. Is it a promotional rate? Note the end date and the rate it drops to, and plan to move the money if the new rate is weak.
  4. Do fees eat the interest? A $5 monthly fee on a $2,000 balance at 4% APY takes $60 of the $80 earned, leaving a net return of 1%.
  5. What is left after tax? Interest in an ordinary account is reported on Form 1099-INT and taxed as income. In the 22% bracket, 4.5% becomes about 3.5% after tax, which is why retirement accounts feature in long-term examples.

Savings growth questions, answered

Does compounding frequency matter?
A little. Daily versus monthly compounding changes results only slightly; the rate and the number of years matter far more.

What about taxes?
Interest in a regular account is usually taxed each year, which slows growth. Retirement accounts can defer or avoid that tax.

Is the Rule of 72 exact?
It's a quick estimate that is most accurate between about 6% and 10%.

What is APY?
APY is the yearly return including compounding. A 5% rate compounded monthly equals about 5.12% APY.

Is 7% a realistic return?
Over long periods the US stock market has averaged roughly 7% after inflation, but returns vary a lot year to year and are not guaranteed.
