A cup of flour and a cup of sugar do not weigh the same. Pick the ingredient and convert grams to cups (or cups to grams) for any recipe.

Why cups and grams don’t match

Cups measure volume and grams measure weight. A cup of flour weighs far less than a cup of honey, so every ingredient needs its own conversion. Professional bakers weigh ingredients because it is more consistent.

How to use the grams to cups converter

  1. Choose the ingredient.
  2. Choose grams → cups or cups → grams.
  3. Enter the amount and press Convert.

The formula

Cups = grams ÷ (grams per cup of that ingredient)

Worked examples

  • 250 g of flour ÷ 120 g per cup = 2.08 cups — about 2 cups plus 1 tablespoon.
  • 100 g of butter = 0.44 cup = about 7 tablespoons.
  • 1 US stick of butter = 1/2 cup = about 113 g.
  • 1 cup of granulated sugar = about 200 g.

US, metric, imperial and Japanese cups compared

Before converting grams, check whose cup the recipe means. The differences are small for a splash of milk and real for flour in bread dough.

A metric cup holds about 5.7% more than a US cup, so a metric recipe asking for 4 cups of flour wants roughly 4 1/4 US cups. Spoons differ too: an Australian tablespoon is 20 ml, a US tablespoon about 15 ml.

How you fill the cup changes the weight

Scooping flour straight from the bag packs it down and can add 20–25% more. For the 120 g figure, spoon the flour into the cup and level it with a knife. Brown sugar is the opposite — recipes expect it packed firmly.

Common mistakes

  • Using one conversion for everything (such as “1 cup = 250 g”).
  • Mixing metric and US cups: a metric cup is 250 ml, a US cup is about 237 ml.
  • Converting salt like sugar: table salt is much denser — 1 cup is about 288 g.

Converting a recipe step by step

Start with the ingredients that matter most for texture — flour, sugar and butter — and convert them by weight. Small amounts such as baking soda, salt and spices can stay in teaspoons, because a kitchen scale is not precise at a few grams.

If a converted recipe turns out dry or dense, the flour was probably packed into the cup. Next time spoon it in and level it, or weigh it, and the result will match the original much more closely.

A European cake recipe converted line by line

A typical continental recipe lists 500 g flour, 250 g sugar, 200 g butter, 300 ml milk and an oven at 180 °C. Line by line:

  • 500 g all-purpose flour ÷ 120 g per cup = 4.17 cups, so 4 cups plus about 2 2/3 tablespoons, spooned and leveled.
  • 250 g granulated sugar ÷ 200 g = exactly 1 1/4 cups.
  • 200 g butter ÷ 227 g = 0.88 cup, which is 14 tablespoons, or 1 3/4 US sticks.
  • 300 ml milk ÷ 236.6 ml = 1.27 cups, so 1 1/4 cups plus a teaspoon.
  • 180 °C = 356 °F; set the oven to 350 °F.

Keep baking powder, salt and vanilla in teaspoons; a few grams is below what most kitchen scales read reliably. Eggs stay as eggs. If the result bakes dense, the flour was packed into the cup; the recipe's 500 g is the number that was right all along.

Baking conversion questions from home cooks

Is a kitchen scale worth it?
For baking, yes. Weighing removes the packing problem and makes recipes repeatable.

How many grams is a teaspoon of sugar?
About 4 g.

How many grams is 1 cup of flour?
About 120–125 g for all-purpose flour, depending on how it is measured.

Why do recipes in grams work better?
Weight does not change with packing or humidity, so results are more consistent.
