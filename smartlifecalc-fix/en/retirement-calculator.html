Will your 401(k), IRA and other savings be enough? This retirement calculator projects your balance at retirement, shows it in today’s dollars, and estimates the income it could support using the popular 4% rule.

Turning a retirement balance into a plan

A retirement projection has two halves: how much you may have saved by retirement, and how much income that balance can safely provide. This calculator estimates the first with compound growth and the second with the widely used 4% guideline.

How to use the retirement calculator

  1. Enter your current age and planned retirement age.
  2. Enter what you have saved and what you add each month.
  3. Adjust the expected return and inflation.
  4. Press Calculate.

The formula

Safe first-year withdrawal ≈ 4% of savings at retirement

Worked example

Age 35, $50,000 saved, $600 a month added, 6% average return until 65. The projection is about $904,000. Using the 4% guideline, that could support roughly $36,000 a year in the first year of retirement, adjusted for inflation after that — before Social Security or any pension.

Remember that $904,000 in 30 years will buy less than it does today. At 3% inflation, it is worth about $370,000 in today's dollars. Look at the inflation-adjusted figure when you judge whether it is enough.

2026 contribution limits and the new Roth catch-up rule

For 2026 the IRS limit on employee deferrals to a 401(k), 403(b) or most 457 plans is $24,500. Workers 50 and older can add a catch-up of $8,000, and those aged 60 to 63 may contribute a higher catch-up of $11,250 if their plan allows it. The IRA limit is $7,500, with a $1,100 catch-up at 50 and over.

New this year under the SECURE 2.0 Act: catch-up contributions from workers whose prior-year wages from that employer exceeded about $150,000 must go into a Roth (after-tax) account. If the plan has no Roth option, those workers cannot make catch-ups at all, so check with your plan administrator.

To see the ceiling in the calculator, enter $2,042 a month for the base limit ($24,500 ÷ 12) or $2,708 with the standard catch-up, then add any employer match on top; the match does not count against the employee limit.

What the 4% guideline means

It comes from studies of historical U.S. market returns, which found that withdrawing about 4% in the first year and raising the amount with inflation lasted around 30 years in most past periods. It is a starting point, not a guarantee; longer retirements or poor early returns may call for a lower rate.

Ways to improve the result

  • Capture the employer match in a 401(k) — it's often the best return you can get.
  • Raise contributions by 1% each year or each time you get a raise.
  • Use tax-advantaged accounts (401(k), IRA, Roth IRA) for tax-deferred or tax-free growth.
  • Work a little longer – it adds contributions, adds growth and shortens the years you need to fund.

Two things to double-check

  • Always take the full employer 401(k) match — it is free money.

Social Security at 62, 67 or 70: one benefit, three sizes

For anyone born in 1960 or later, full retirement age is 67. Claiming early reduces the benefit by 5/9 of 1% per month for the first 36 months and 5/12 of 1% for each month beyond that; waiting past 67 adds 8% a year until 70. On a $2,000 full-age benefit:

Retirement planning questions answered

How much do I need?
A common estimate is 70–80% of pre-retirement income, from all sources combined.

What return should I enter?
Use a conservative figure (for example 5–6% for a balanced portfolio) and test a lower one to see the range.

Is Social Security included?
No. Check your estimate at ssa.gov and add it to the income from savings.

What is the 4% rule?
Research found that withdrawing 4% of savings in the first year, then adjusting for inflation, historically lasted about 30 years in most cases.

How much do I need to retire?
A common guideline is 25 times your expected yearly spending, or 70–80% of your pre-retirement income.
