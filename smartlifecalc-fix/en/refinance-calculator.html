Refinancing can lower your payment when rates drop, but closing costs mean it only pays off if you stay long enough. This calculator shows your monthly savings and how many months it takes to break even.

Is refinancing worth it? Look at the break-even point

Refinancing replaces your current mortgage with a new one, usually to get a lower rate, change the term or take cash out. It is never free: closing costs commonly run 2–5% of the loan. The key question is how many months of lower payments it takes to earn those costs back. That is the break-even point, and this calculator shows it directly.

How to use the refinance calculator

  1. Enter your current balance, rate and years left.
  2. Enter the new rate and term you were offered.
  3. Enter the estimated closing costs.
  4. Press Should I Refinance?

The formula

Break-even months = closing costs ÷ monthly savings

Worked example

You owe $300,000 at 7% with 28 years left; your payment is about $2,039. A new 30-year loan at 6% would be about $1,799 — roughly $240 a month less. With $6,000 in closing costs, break-even is about 25 months. If you plan to stay at least three years, the refinance probably pays off.

Be careful: starting a new 30-year clock adds two years of payments. If you choose a new loan that matches your remaining 28 years, the payment is about $1,845 — still $194 less — and you don't extend your debt.

When refinancing usually makes sense

  • The new rate is meaningfully lower (often 0.75–1 point or more) and you'll stay past break-even.
  • You want to drop PMI after your home has risen in value.
  • You want to move from an adjustable rate to a fixed rate before it resets.
  • You want a shorter term to pay the house off sooner.

When it often doesn't

  • You may sell or move before break-even.
  • You're far into the loan and most of each payment already goes to principal.
  • The "no-cost" offer simply adds the costs to the rate or balance — compare the APR.

Worth knowing

  • Resetting to a new 30-year term lowers the payment but can increase total interest.

Six numbers to check on every Loan Estimate

Lenders must give you a Loan Estimate within three business days of your application, and the form is standardized so offers line up.

  1. Rate and rate lock. Confirm the rate is locked and for how many days; an expired lock can cost you the quoted rate.
  2. Section A, origination charges. This is the lender's own fee and the cleanest line to compare between lenders.
  3. Points. One point is 1% of the loan, $3,000 on $300,000. Divide the cost of the points by the monthly saving they create to get their own break-even.
  4. Rolled-in costs. If $6,000 of costs is added to the 30-year 6% loan above, the payment becomes $1,835 instead of $1,799, and the monthly saving falls from $240 to $204 for the life of the loan.
  5. Prepayment penalty. The yes-or-no box on page one should say No if you plan to pay extra or sell early.
  6. Cash to close. Prepaid interest and the new escrow deposit are not lender fees, but they are real cash due at closing, partly offset later by the refund of your old escrow balance.

Homeowners' questions about refinancing

Should I pay points?
Points lower the rate in exchange for cash now. Divide the cost of the points by the monthly saving they create to find their own break-even.

Does refinancing hurt my credit?
The credit check causes a small, temporary dip. Shopping several lenders within a short window is usually treated as one inquiry.

Is cash-out refinancing a good idea?
It can fund major repairs at a low rate, but it turns home equity into debt secured by your house.

How much does the rate need to drop?
A common rule of thumb is about 0.75–1 percentage point, but the break-even calculation is more reliable.

What are typical closing costs?
Often 2–5% of the loan amount, including appraisal, title and lender fees.
