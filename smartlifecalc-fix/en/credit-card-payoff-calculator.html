Credit card interest is one of the most expensive debts there is. Paying only the minimum can stretch a balance out for many years. Use this calculator to see exactly when you will be debt-free and how much interest you will pay.

Why paying a little more changes everything

Credit card interest is charged on the balance you carry each month. At typical rates above 20%, a payment that only slightly exceeds the interest barely moves the balance. That is why the minimum payment can keep you in debt for years. This calculator shows how long payoff takes and how much interest you pay at the amount you choose.

The formula

Each month: new balance = balance + balance × (APR ÷ 12) − payment

The 36-month figure on your statement, decoded

Since the Credit CARD Act of 2009, nearly every statement must show how long payoff takes at the minimum and, unless that is already under three years, the monthly amount that clears the balance in 36 months. That box is a ready-made target you can test here.

Take a $4,000 balance at 24% APR. A typical minimum is interest plus 1% of the balance, about $120 at first. Paying a flat $120 every month takes roughly 56 months and costs about $2,660 in interest. The statement's 36-month figure works out to about $157 a month, with total interest near $1,650. That extra $37 a month cuts 20 months off the payoff and saves about $1,000.

To check your own statement, enter 36 in the months field and compare the payment returned. Both the statement box and this tool assume no new charges and a fixed APR.

A plan that works

  1. Stop adding new charges to the card you are paying off. Use a debit card for daily spending.
  2. Pick a fixed payment you can keep every month, and set up automatic payment.
  3. Call the issuer and ask for a lower APR. Customers with on-time history are sometimes offered one.
  4. Consider a 0% balance transfer if you can pay it off before the promotion ends. Watch the transfer fee (often 3–5%).
  5. Several cards? Use the snowball or avalanche method to choose which card gets the extra money.

Common mistakes

  • Paying the minimum, which usually shrinks as the balance shrinks — stretching payoff even further.
  • Moving debt to a 0% card and then running the old card back up.
  • Closing old cards right away; it can raise your credit utilization ratio and lower your score.

Myth versus fact on credit card interest

  • Myth: carrying a small balance helps your credit score. Fact: scores reward a low balance relative to your limit on the statement date; paying in full costs you nothing in score terms.
  • Myth: only the old balance accrues interest. Fact: once you carry a balance, most issuers suspend the grace period, so new purchases accrue interest from the day you make them.
  • Myth: the minimum payment is a fixed percentage. Fact: it is usually interest and fees plus about 1% of principal, or a floor such as $25 to $35, whichever is greater, which is why it shrinks as the balance falls.
  • Myth: a 0% balance transfer is free. Fact: the transfer fee of 3% to 5% is added to the balance up front, $180 to $300 on a $6,000 transfer. It often still beats a 22% APR.
  • Myth: every 0% promotion works the same way. Fact: deferred interest offers, common on store cards, charge all the interest back from day one if any balance remains when the promotion ends. A true 0% intro APR does not.

Reader questions about paying down a card balance

How is credit card interest calculated?
Most issuers use the average daily balance and a daily rate (APR ÷ 365). This calculator uses a monthly rate (APR ÷ 12), which gives very close results.

Does paying twice a month help?
Slightly, because the average daily balance is lower. The bigger win is the total amount you pay each month.

Should I pay off cards before saving?
Most planners suggest a small emergency fund first (so new emergencies don't go on the card), then aggressive payoff of high-interest debt.

Why does the minimum payment take so long?
Minimum payments are often about 1–3% of the balance, so most of the payment goes to interest and the balance falls slowly.

Should I pay off the highest APR card first?
Paying the highest APR first (the avalanche method) saves the most money. Try our Debt Payoff Calculator to compare methods.
