Whether it is a personal loan, student loan or small business loan, the first question is always the same: how much will I pay each month, and how much interest in total? This loan calculator answers both and shows a yearly amortization schedule.

How to read your loan results

A personal loan, student loan or small business loan is usually repaid in equal monthly installments. Each payment covers that month's interest first, and whatever is left reduces the balance. Because the balance shrinks, the interest part gets smaller every month and the principal part gets larger. The amortization schedule in this calculator shows that shift month by month, so you can see exactly where your money goes.

The formula

Payment = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n)

The two numbers that matter most

The monthly payment tells you whether the loan fits your budget. The total interest tells you what the loan really costs. A longer term lowers the first number but raises the second, and that trade-off is the most important decision you make when borrowing.

Worked case: the fee that hides inside the APR

The example above borrows $20,000 at 9% for 60 months, giving a payment of $415.17. Now suppose the lender charges a 5% origination fee. Two things can happen, and you can model both here.

  • Fee taken from the proceeds. You receive $19,000 but repay $415.17 a month on $20,000. The yearly cost on the money you actually got works out to about 11.2%, which is close to the APR the lender must disclose under the Truth in Lending Act.
  • Fee added to the loan. To net $20,000 you borrow $21,053. The payment rises to about $437 and total interest to about $5,170, up from $4,910.

Either way the fee costs you roughly $1,000 plus interest on it. A no-fee loan at 10% ($425 a month, $5,496 in interest) is the cheaper deal in both cases. When offers are close, compare APRs rather than note rates, and ask whether the fee is deducted or financed.

Why the rate you are offered can differ

  • Credit score – the single biggest factor for unsecured loans.
  • Debt-to-income ratio – lenders compare your monthly debt payments with your gross income.
  • Fees – an origination fee of 1–8% is often taken out of the loan amount. Compare offers by APR, which includes these fees.
  • Secured vs unsecured – a loan backed by a car or savings account is usually cheaper.

Paying extra: what actually happens

Most installment loans let you pay extra toward principal without penalty. Every extra dollar reduces the balance that interest is charged on, so the savings compound. On the 5-year example above, adding $50 a month shortens the loan by about 7 months and saves several hundred dollars. Before paying extra, check the loan agreement for a prepayment penalty and ask the lender to apply the money to principal, not to next month's payment.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing only monthly payments between offers with different terms.
  • Ignoring the origination fee: a $20,000 loan with a 5% fee only puts $19,000 in your account.
  • Borrowing for longer than the thing you are buying lasts.

Two things to double-check

  • A shorter term means a higher payment but much less interest.
  • Even a 1% lower rate can save hundreds or thousands of dollars.

Borrowers' questions, answered

Is this the same formula banks use?
Yes. Fixed-rate installment loans use the standard amortization formula: payment = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n), where r is the monthly rate and n the number of months.

Why is my first payment different?
Interest is charged by the day, so a first period longer or shorter than a month changes the first payment slightly.

Should I consolidate several debts into one loan?
Only if the new APR is clearly lower and you stop adding to the old balances. See the debt snowball and credit card payoff calculators to compare.

What is amortization?
Amortization means each equal payment covers that month’s interest first and the rest pays down the balance, so the interest part shrinks over time.

Does this include fees?
No. Origination fees or insurance are not included. Add them to the loan amount if they are financed.
