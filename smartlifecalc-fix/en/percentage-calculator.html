The formula

X% of Y = Y × X ÷ 100  |  Percent change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100

Everyday examples

  • Sales: a $120 jacket at 25% off saves $30, so you pay $90.
  • Raises: going from $52,000 to $54,600 is a 5% raise.
  • Tips: 18% of a $64 bill is $11.52.
  • Price increases: a bill that rose from $150 to $165 went up 10%.

Discount today, markup needed to get back to the original price

A percentage cut and the percentage rise that undoes it are never the same number, because the second one is measured from a smaller base. The gain needed to recover from a loss of d is d ÷ (1 − d).

Value falls byGain needed to get back

25%33.3%

Example: a stock bought at $80 drops 40% to $48. Getting back to $80 means rising $32 on a $48 base, which is 66.7%, not 40%. The same logic explains why a portfolio that loses 50% and then gains 50% ends at 75% of where it started: $100 → $50 → $75. For a shopper, a store that marks a jacket down 25% and later raises the sale price by 25% is still selling it below the original tag: $120 → $90 → $112.50.

Mistakes that trip people up

Up and down are not symmetrical. If a price rises 20% and then falls 20%, you don't return to the start: $100 → $120 → $96. Percent vs percentage points: a rate moving from 4% to 5% is a 1 percentage point increase, but a 25% relative increase. Stacked discounts multiply: 30% off and then another 10% off is 37% off in total, not 40%.

Mental-math shortcuts

  • 10% → move the decimal one place (10% of $84 = $8.40).
  • 5% → half of 10%. 20% → double 10%.
  • X% of Y equals Y% of X — 8% of 50 is the same as 50% of 8 = 4.

Small details that change the result

  • A 50% drop needs a 100% gain to recover.

Yearly percent changes compound; they do not simply add

When a change repeats, multiply the growth factors instead of adding the percentages.

  • Rent rising 5% a year for six years: 1.05 raised to the sixth power is 1.34, a 34% total increase, not 30%. A $1,500 rent becomes about $2,010.
  • Inflation of 3%, 4% and 3% over three years: 1.03 × 1.04 × 1.03 = 1.103, so prices are up 10.3%, not 10%.
  • An investment up 50% over five years grew about 8.45% a year, not 10%, because 1.0845 compounded for five years is about 1.50. The Investor.gov compound interest calculator lets you check this kind of figure.
  • To undo a 25% increase, divide by 1.25 rather than subtracting 25%: $125 ÷ 1.25 = $100, while $125 × 0.75 = $93.75.

The percent change option above handles one step at a time. For a multi-year question, run each step on the previous result, or multiply the factors as shown and subtract 1 at the end.

Percentage puzzles readers ask about

How do I find the original price before a discount?
Divide the sale price by (1 − discount). $90 after 25% off: 90 ÷ 0.75 = $120.

How do I add sales tax?
Multiply by (1 + rate). $50 with 8% tax: 50 × 1.08 = $54.

How do I calculate a discount?
Use “What is X% of Y?” to get the discount amount, then subtract it from the price.

How do I reverse a percentage?
If a price after a 20% discount is $80, the original is 80 ÷ (1 − 0.20) = $100.
