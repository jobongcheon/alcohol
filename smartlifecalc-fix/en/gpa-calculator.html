How GPA is calculated

Your GPA is a credit-weighted average. Each letter grade is turned into grade points (A = 4.0, B = 3.0 and so on), multiplied by the course's credit hours, and the total is divided by the total credits. A 4-credit class therefore moves your GPA more than a 1-credit class.

The formula

GPA = Σ (grade points × credits) ÷ Σ credits

Worked case: from 3.2 at 60 credits to a 3.5 at graduation

Grade points, not GPAs, are what add up. A 3.2 over 60 credits is 192 grade points. A 3.5 over 120 credits requires 420. The remaining 60 credits must therefore supply 228 points, an average of 3.80.

A 3.80 average is roughly two A's for every A- or B+, every term, so a 3.5 is possible but leaves no slack; a 3.4 is a more realistic plan. One shortcut exists at some schools: grade replacement. Retaking a 4-credit course graded D (1.0) and earning a B (3.0) adds 8 points to the 60 credits already on the record, lifting 192 ÷ 60 = 3.20 to 200 ÷ 60 = 3.33 in a single term. Check your catalog, since other schools average both attempts or cap how many courses can be replaced.

Cumulative GPA

To combine semesters, don't average the GPAs — weight them by credits. If you had a 3.4 over 30 credits and earn a 3.0 over 15 credits, your cumulative GPA is (3.4 × 30 + 3.0 × 15) ÷ 45 = 3.27.

How much can one semester raise your GPA?

The more credits you've completed, the harder it is to move. After 90 credits at 2.8, a perfect 4.0 over 15 credits only raises you to about 2.97. Planning early matters; so does knowing your school's retake policy, since some schools replace the old grade.

Weighted vs unweighted (high school)

Many high schools add a point for honors, AP or IB courses (an A counts as 5.0). Colleges often recalculate GPA their own way, so report what your school's transcript shows.

Where a GPA threshold actually bites: federal aid, scholarships, applications

  • Federal student aid. Schools must check satisfactory academic progress. Under federal rules, by the end of the second academic year a student must have at least a C average (2.0 on this scale) or standing consistent with graduating, and must be completing courses fast enough to finish within 150% of the program length. Falling short can suspend Pell Grants and federal loans until an appeal succeeds or grades recover.
  • Merit scholarships. Renewal minimums are commonly set at 3.0 on the cumulative GPA, so one weak 15-credit term after a 3.1 start can cost the award.
  • Transcripts and applications. Point values differ by school: some give an A+ 4.3, others 4.0; an A- may be 3.67 or 3.7. Graduate and professional programs often recalculate on their own scale, sometimes on the last 60 credits only, so report what your transcript shows and let them convert.

GPA questions from students and parents

Do pass/fail courses count?
Usually not in the GPA, though they count toward credits.

What is a good GPA?
It depends on the goal: many scholarships and graduate programs look for 3.0–3.5 or higher.

What is the difference between weighted and unweighted GPA?
Unweighted GPA uses the 4.0 scale above. Weighted GPA adds extra points for honors or AP classes, up to about 5.0.
